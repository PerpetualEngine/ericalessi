The ice cooler nobody opened

Five hours, six people, a house with a garden that turned out to matter more than I planned for.

Every week I send one of these.

When I teach, the entry has two halves. What I carried into the room, and what people carried out of it. Those two things are rarely the same, and the distance between them has turned out to be the most interesting thing I have found in teaching so far.

This is the first one.

What I brought

Centres of gravity. I wanted people to widen their attention past the point of contact and start tracking where the weight actually lives, in themselves and in the person they are dancing with. Alongside that, grounding. The feet, the heels, the sense of being connected downward rather than perched on top of the floor.

Few exercises across five hours, each one long. Bringing energy up from the ground, then consolidating it on one or two points of contact and staying there until tone arrives in the body. Statues that reshape each other in pairs, then in threes, with one rule: two centres of gravity stay in contact somehow. A game with balls and animals that I will explain another time. And the floor flip, which I was most curious about: lying down as an act of holding the space above you, so the floor stops being the thing underneath and starts being the ceiling.

I got through all of it.

What I was thinking about when it ended

Group size, mostly.

Six people is too few for five hours. If two step out to rest, four are left carrying the energy of the room, and four cannot hold five hours. I want two more as ballast. Eight is the number. Six to eight from now on.

Then the shape of the day. The jam arrived too late. Next time it comes in earlier, probably one in the middle and another at the end if there is time. The sharing in the middle helped more than I expected, so there should be more than one.

And longer breaks, which is the second session running where I have under-budgeted everything that is not an exercise. Last time I wanted more warm-up. This time I wanted more space. Two data points make a pattern, so from here I plan the pauses first and fit the material around them.

The floor flip landed. One dancer named it as the high point of his day and described it better than I taught it:

"It's totally different if you lay down thinking that you are laying down on the ground, or if you are laying down because you are holding a bigger space. Generally when I enter a jam I use the floor a lot to be in contact with something and feel myself, but I never thought about just reversing the sensation."

What they were thinking about

I record the closing sharing now, with everyone's permission and no names.

I walked out of that room doing arithmetic about group sizes. Here is what they walked out with:

"Thank you for your caring and loving energy. You wanted everything to be just right. You bought a whole ice cooler that we just forgot about. It's in the corner. You filled it with ice because it could be hot. This really special care taken. It felt like a beautiful laboratory, well thought out."

"It's your heart that can be felt from a distance. The passion, the love, the dedication that you bring into the space is felt."

Nobody drank from that cooler. It sat in the corner all day with the ice melting in it, and it turned out to be the thing one person reached for first when asked what the day was.

I was evaluating a structure. They were describing a person.

That is the gap, and I would never have found it on my own, because the last thing you can see from inside your own teaching is whether people felt held.

What they found that I could not have planned

One found the ground as a partner rather than a surface:

"I never thought about the ground like that. It's a relation between the ground and me. I can use the ground as a partner. I'm supporting it and it's supporting me. It was like, wow. It opened the whole space and many possibilities."

One found a direction he avoids:

"There was a point where I was like, oh wow, I'm not really dancing with this horizontal axis, because of a fear of falling. I don't really open my body in that way. I just noticed that."

One connected her own grounding to how safe her partners feel, which is a link I have never heard a student make unprompted:

"Sometimes I'm missing a bit of stability in my feet, and then the lifts are a bit wobbly and people don't feel so safe. So really connecting back with the heels, with the sense of okay, I'm grounded to the earth, brought a lot more stability in my body."

One described exactly the thing I hope people take from a score, which is that a single constraint changes everything downstream of it:

"How the dance changes when I'm just connecting with my chest, always trying to find it again even if there's a disconnection and coming back to that. I always find that fascinating, when I have one thing I'm playing with like a score."

One who had been away from workshops for a long time found language for what her body already knew:

"A lot of things I feel like I'm doing quite intuitively in the body by now. I liked getting the mental concept again, like, ah yes, that's a repositioning of the body."

One got something out of a silly game with balls and animals that I did not put in it:

"It helped me release some relationship tension."

And one spent half an hour losing to the garden:

"At the beginning it was hard to focus, I have to admit, because outside was so beautiful, so full of detail, that I surprised myself. I was playing with a butterfly, a yellow butterfly, for the whole half an hour. I was listening, but she was calling me a lot."

He was apologetic about it. I think it is the most honest thing anyone said all day. He then connected it to Feldenkrais and peripheral vision, and to how much we over-trust the eyes:

"If you change perspective, things change with the sight. But if I touch somebody and I feel this body, I cannot be fooled."

The one place we agreed

I came out of the day wanting longer breaks. Then, near the end of the sharing, without knowing that, one dancer said:

"It was a lot of information for me. Processing. Sometimes it was intense for the body. I felt I needed something to stop and write."

Both halves of the room arrived at the same fix from opposite directions. That happens less often than the gap does, and when it happens you should probably just do the thing.

One thing to practise

Lie down on the floor. Give your weight completely, the way you always do.

Now keep your body exactly as it is and change one thing in your mind. You are holding the space above you.

Stay a few minutes and notice your ribs, your breath, the front of your body. Then get up and dance with it.

A question to carry

Which axis do you avoid, and what are you afraid would happen if you opened it?

You probably already know. Most of us have one direction we quietly never go.

In presence,
Eric
